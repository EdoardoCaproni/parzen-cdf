\large Parzen window and neural network: method, measurements, and limits}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent Given a finite sample and no assumption about the distribution behind it, we
estimate the cumulative distribution function with a small neural network trained on
Parzen-window labels, and obtain the density by differentiating it. The report is
self-contained: every quantitative claim states how it was obtained, and the scripts that
produce each number are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:repro}.

Three choices distinguish this version of the work from its predecessors, and each is
argued rather than asserted. First, the window width is chosen by cross-validation rather
than by a rule of thumb: we show that a rule of the form $h_1 = c\,\shat$ cannot exist,
because the optimal constant varies by more than a factor of ten across distributions, and
that recalibrating it on a larger benchmark makes matters worse rather than better. Second,
the network is an architecture whose output is a valid distribution function for every
value of its parameters, so that monotonicity, the limits at infinity, non-negativity of
the density and unit mass are guaranteed by construction instead of being repaired
afterwards on a grid. Third, since on the data the method is meant for the true
distribution is unknown, quality is reported through quantities computable from the sample
alone; we measured which of those quantities are informative, and found that the most
natural one is actively misleading.

We also state a limitation openly: a mixture of logistic distribution functions has
strictly positive density on the whole line, and therefore rounds off sharp edges. On
distributions with compact support the error grows by a factor between three and fourteen.
We checked whether more capacity repairs this, and it does not come for free.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================================

\subsection{The problem}

We are given $n$ observations $x_1,\dots,x_n$, drawn independently from a distribution
$F$ that we know nothing about, and we are asked for two things: an estimate $\Fhat$ of the
distribution function, and an estimate $\fhat$ of the density, obtained by differentiating
the first.

The order matters, and it is not incidental. One could estimate the density directly and
integrate it; going the other way round --- estimating $F$ first and differentiating --- is
what the assignment prescribes, and it also has a technical justification worth stating
early. A distribution function is a smoother object than a density: it is an integral, and
integration averages away exactly the kind of local fluctuation that makes density
estimation hard. A finite sample produces a jagged density estimate but a rather well
behaved cumulative one. The price is paid at the end, when differentiating brings the
fluctuation back, and much of this report is about paying that price as little as possible.

There is a second reason, specific to the neural approach. Regressing a curve that is
bounded between $0$ and $1$, monotone, and asymptotically flat is a far better conditioned
problem than regressing a density, which is unbounded above, has no natural scale, and must
integrate to one. If the network is asked for a distribution function, its structure can be
made to guarantee all the properties we want; if it is asked for a density, guaranteeing
non-negativity and unit mass at the same time is considerably harder.

\subsection{What is delivered}

The pipeline takes a vector of numbers and returns:

\begin{itemize}
  \item two functions, $\Fhat$ and $\fhat$, evaluable at any real point --- not tables
        sampled on a grid;
  \item a window width $\hhat$, reported also as $\hhat_1 = \hhat\sqrt{n}$ so that it fits
        the schedule $h_n = h_1/\sqrt{n}$;
  \item a block of diagnostics computable without knowing the true distribution;
  \item a command-line entry point, so that the procedure can be run by someone who did not
        write it, on data we have never seen.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{How to read this document}

Every quantitative statement is accompanied by how it was obtained: on how many
distributions, over how many repetitions, and with which script. Proofs are collected in
Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} so that the main text stays readable; statements and consequences
appear where they are used. Appendix~\ref{app:repro} maps each experiment to the file that
reproduces it.

Several sections end with an explicit list of what we are \emph{not} claiming. These are
deliberate. During this work a number of plausible claims turned out to be false when
measured, including some of our own, and the habit of writing down the boundary of a result
proved more useful than the result itself.

\subsection{Where this work comes from}

The project went through three passes, and the reason the present one exists is worth
telling, because it explains several choices that would otherwise look arbitrary.

The \textbf{first pass} built the Parzen-window estimator and chose its window with
Silverman's rule of thumb. The rule is standard, but its constant is derived for a Gaussian
kernel, that is, for a kernel with unit standard deviation. The kernel actually in use is
the logistic one, whose standard deviation is $\pi/\sqrt3 \approx 1.814$. Applying the rule
without correcting for this oversmooths by exactly that factor. It is a small oversight with
a visible cost: on the reference trimodal mixture at $n = 500$, averaged over ten samples,
the uncorrected rule gives a Kolmogorov--Smirnov distance of $0.0628$ against $0.0449$ for
the corrected one, and an integrated squared error on the density of $0.0334$ against
$0.0154$ --- more than a factor of two.

The \textbf{second pass} fixed the scale, adopted the schedule $h_n = h_1/\sqrt{n}$, and
looked for a rule of thumb for the remaining constant, settling on $h_1 = 1.5\,\shat$. It
also introduced the neural stage, trained on Parzen labels, with monotonicity repaired
downstream by taking a running maximum of the estimated curve and rescaling it to $[0,1]$.

The \textbf{third pass} is this one. It began as a review and turned into a redesign,
because three things did not survive checking. The rule for $h_1$ was calibrated and
validated on the same small family of distributions, which measures how well one has
interpolated one's own benchmark rather than how well the rule generalises. The network's
guarantees were obtained after the fact, on a grid whose extent was taken from the true
distribution --- an object that does not exist when the method is applied to real data. And
the pipeline had no way of reading a sample from a file at all.

Two consequences for the reader. First, this report re-derives everything from the
beginning and does not rely on the earlier write-ups; where an earlier conclusion is
superseded, we say so and give the measurement that supersedes it. Second, several of the
numbers reported in the earlier passes are not reproducible as stated, for a reason
explained in Section~\ref{sec:reproducibility}: a defect in how random seeds were applied
meant that runs labelled with different seeds were not actually controlled by them. That
defect has been fixed, and every figure in this report comes from runs that can be
reproduced.

\section{Development data: generating honest samples}
% =====================================================================================

\subsection{Why the generator matters even though it is not part of the pipeline}

The estimator never sees the generator. It receives a vector of numbers and knows nothing
about where they came from, which is the whole point of a nonparametric method. It would
therefore be tempting to treat the way we produce test data as an implementation detail.

It is not, for a simple reason: every number in this report was measured on samples we
generated ourselves. Calibration of the window, the choice of the number of network
components, the comparison between architectures --- all of it rests on synthetic data drawn
from distributions whose truth we know, because otherwise there would be nothing to compare
against. A generator with a subtle bias would contaminate every one of those conclusions,
and it would do so silently: nothing downstream would look wrong. The generator is not in
the pipeline, but it is upstream of every measurement, and it deserves the same scrutiny as
the estimator.

\subsection{Ancestral sampling from a mixture}

Our test distributions are mixtures: densities of the form
\begin{equation}
f(x) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{k} w_i\, f_i(x), \qquad w_i \ge 0, \quad \sum_i w_i = 1 ,
\label{eq:mixture}
\end{equation}
where the components $f_i$ are ordinary densities, typically normal. Mixtures are convenient
because their distribution function is the same weighted sum of the component distribution
functions, so the reference curves against which we score are available in closed form.

A mixture is best understood not as a formula but as a two-stage random process, with a
hidden variable: first draw which component will generate the point, then draw the point
from that component. Sampling by following those two stages in order is called
\emph{ancestral sampling}, a name borrowed from graphical models, where one draws each
variable after its parents. In our case the parent is the component index $I$ and the child
is the observation $X$:
\[
I \sim \text{Categorical}(w_1,\dots,w_k), \qquad X \mid I = i \;\sim\; f_i .
\]

This is not an approximation of the mixture; it \emph{is} the mixture, and the verification
is one line:
\[
\Pr(X \le x) \;=\; \sum_i \Pr(I = i)\, \Pr(X \le x \mid I = i) \;=\; \sum_i w_i F_i(x),
\]
which is the mixture distribution function by definition. There is no tolerance, no
convergence, no numerical error --- and the cost is $O(n)$, one categorical draw and one
component draw per observation, both vectorised.

The alternative would be \emph{inverse transform sampling}: if $U$ is uniform on $(0,1)$ then
$F^{-1}(U)$ has distribution function $F$, for any $F$ whatsoever. It is the universal
method, and for a mixture it is also the wrong one: the distribution function of a mixture of
normals cannot be inverted in closed form, so each sample would require a numerical root
search. The result would be exact only up to the tolerance of that search, and would cost
$O(n \times \text{iterations})$. We implemented it anyway and compared: the two samples are
statistically indistinguishable (two-sample Kolmogorov--Smirnov test, $p = 0.696$). The
choice is therefore not between right and wrong, but between exact-and-cheap and
approximate-and-expensive.

\subsection{The Ziggurat algorithm}

Ancestral sampling reduces the problem to drawing from a single normal component, which
leaves the question of how a normal variate is produced from uniform random bits. This is a
classical problem with several classical answers, and it is worth a paragraph because the
efficiency of the whole simulation depends on it.

The obvious approach, inverting the normal distribution function, requires evaluating a
special function for every sample. The Box--Muller transform produces two normal variates
from two uniforms using a logarithm, a square root and a pair of trigonometric functions;
Marsaglia's polar method removes the trigonometry at the cost of a rejection step. All of
them are exact; all of them are relatively expensive per sample.

The \emph{Ziggurat} method (Marsaglia and Tsang, 2000) is a rejection sampler designed so
that the expensive branch is almost never taken. Consider the right half of the bell curve
and cover it with a stack of horizontal strips --- around 128 or 256 of them --- chosen to
all have \emph{exactly the same area}, plus a tail region at the bottom with that same area.
The picture resembles a stepped Mesopotamian ziggurat, which is where the name comes from.
Because the strips have equal area, choosing one uniformly at random is a matter of masking a
few random bits.

Within a strip, the density curve enters from one side: part of the rectangle lies entirely
underneath the curve, and a thin fringe near the edge sticks out above it. The algorithm
draws a strip index, draws a horizontal coordinate uniformly inside the strip, and then
checks a single inequality: if the point falls in the safe region it is accepted immediately,
with no evaluation of the exponential at all. Only if it falls in the fringe does the
algorithm evaluate the density and accept with the appropriate probability; and only if the
bottom strip is drawn does it fall back to a separate routine for the tail. With 256 strips
the fast branch covers roughly 98--99\% of draws, which is why the method costs little more
than one uniform and one comparison per sample.

The point that matters for us is that this is a rejection sampler, and rejection sampling is
\emph{exact}: the accepted points are distributed precisely according to the target density,
because the procedure is equivalent to sampling uniformly under the curve and keeping the
abscissa. The speed is not bought with accuracy, and there is no approximation to declare.

Two clarifications, since the two levels are easy to confuse. First, the Ziggurat is not
something we implemented: it is the algorithm NumPy uses internally when it draws a
standard normal variate. We verified this rather than assuming it --- the
documentation strings do not mention the algorithm, but the compiled library that
\texttt{Generator} links against exports symbols named after it, among them the tail
cut-off constant of the normal Ziggurat and its reciprocal, while the legacy
\texttt{RandomState} module exports no such symbol, consistent with its use of the polar
method. Second, ancestral sampling and the Ziggurat are
not alternatives: they sit at different levels and compose. Ancestral sampling decides
\emph{which} normal to draw from; the Ziggurat is how each individual normal variate is
actually produced.

What can and cannot be verified should be stated separately. That NumPy implements the
Ziggurat correctly is not something we can prove; what we can check, and what actually
matters, is that the output has the properties we rely on: that it follows the intended
distribution, that the components appear with the intended probabilities, and that
successive draws are independent.

The second check deserves a comment, because it is the one usually omitted. A correct
generator should not merely \emph{pass} a goodness-of-fit test; the $p$-values it produces
should be \emph{uniformly distributed}. A generator whose $p$-values cluster near one would
be as suspicious as one that rejects too often, since it would mean the samples fit the
target better than chance allows. Testing the uniformity of the $p$-values is therefore the
right check, and it is the one we ran.

One further check concerns us directly. Every experiment in this report averages over
repetitions obtained with seeds $0, 1, 2, \dots$, and if nearby seeds produced correlated
streams then those repetitions would not be independent and every reported average would be
optimistic. With 300 replicates a few borderline values appeared, so we repeated the test
with 2000:

The earlier borderline values were fluctuations. As an independent confirmation, the average
of the 2000 sample means is $0.69930$ against a true mixture mean of $0.70000$, a discrepancy
of about a third of a standard error.

\subsection{A note on circularity}

There is a tempting argument that we deliberately do not make. It runs: the generator never
evaluates the distribution function --- ancestral sampling and the Ziggurat both avoid it ---
therefore estimating $F$ from the samples is not circular.

The argument does not hold, and it is worth explaining why, because it sounds convincing.
Samples drawn independently from $F$ carry no information about $F$ beyond the statistical
information any such sample would carry, regardless of the algorithm that produced them. Had
we sampled by inverting the distribution function, the resulting $x_i$ would be
indistinguishable from ours, and no estimator could exploit the difference. There is no
leakage to eliminate, so there is nothing for this argument to defend against.

What does need to be established is a different and simpler statement: that the estimator
sees the sample and nothing else. That is a property of the code, verifiable by reading it,
and Section~\ref{sec:delivery} explains how the present design makes it structural rather
than a matter of discipline.

\takeaway{The generator is not part of the estimation pipeline, but every measurement in
this report depends on it, so it was checked rather than assumed. Ancestral sampling is
exact by construction and costs $O(n)$; the Ziggurat, which produces the individual normal
variates, is an exact rejection sampler and not a fast approximation. The two operate at
different levels and compose.}

\section{The Parzen window estimator}
% =====================================================================================

\subsection{Definition and intuition}

The Parzen window estimator replaces each observation by a small bump and averages them.
Given a kernel $K$ --- a density, usually symmetric and centred at zero --- and a window
width $h > 0$, the density estimate is
\begin{equation}
\fhat(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{n h} \sum_{i=1}^{n} k\!\left(\frac{x - x_i}{h}\right),
\label{eq:parzen-pdf}
\end{equation}
where $k$ is the kernel density. The intuition is that a sample tells us where probability
mass is, but says nothing about how it is spread in between; the kernel supplies that missing
smoothness, and $h$ decides how much of it.

The window is the only real choice. Too small, and the estimate degenerates towards a
collection of spikes at the observations, reproducing the sample rather than the
distribution; too large, and genuine structure is smoothed away, with separate modes merging
into one. Everything else --- the shape of the kernel, the details of the implementation ---
matters far less. Chapter~\ref{sec:window} is devoted to this single number.

\subsection{The logistic kernel and a closed-form distribution function}

Since what we want first is the distribution function rather than the density, it pays to
choose a kernel whose antiderivative we know. Integrating~\eqref{eq:parzen-pdf} gives
\begin{equation}
\Fhat(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} K\!\left(\frac{x - x_i}{h}\right),
\label{eq:parzen-cdf}
\end{equation}
with $K$ the kernel's own distribution function. For the \emph{logistic} kernel that
function is the sigmoid, $K(u) = 1/(1 + e^{-u})$, and the estimate becomes a plain average of
sigmoids: smooth, strictly increasing, bounded between $0$ and $1$, and available in closed
form at any point. Its derivative is exactly~\eqref{eq:parzen-pdf}, so the density and the
distribution function are consistent by construction rather than by numerical
differentiation. We verified this to machine precision: the largest discrepancy between the
analytic kernel density and the numerical derivative of the sigmoid is $1.3 \times 10^{-8}$
over a fine grid.

That the estimate is a sum of sigmoids will matter again in Chapter~\ref{sec:network}, where
the chosen network architecture turns out to have exactly the same form.

The choice of kernel carries a subtlety that caused a real error in the first pass of this
project. Kernels differ in how much they smooth at a given $h$, because they have different
spreads:

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{@{}lc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Kernel} & \textbf{Standard deviation} \\
\midrule
logistic & $\pi/\sqrt{3} \approx 1.8138$ \\
Gaussian & $1$ \\
box (uniform) & $1/\sqrt{3} \approx 0.5774$ \\
Epanechnikov & $1/\sqrt{5} \approx 0.4472$ \\
triangular & $1/\sqrt{6} \approx 0.4082$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{The available kernels and their spreads. Classical bandwidth rules assume the
Gaussian row.}
\label{tab:kernels}
\end{table}

Every classical bandwidth rule --- Silverman's, Scott's, the plug-in family --- is derived
for a kernel of unit standard deviation. Using such a rule with the logistic kernel and no
correction therefore smooths $1.814$ times more than intended. The fix is to divide the
recommended width by the kernel's standard deviation, so that the two kernels smooth
equivalently; we refer to this below as \emph{variance matching}. The cost of omitting it is
not academic: on the reference trimodal mixture at $n = 500$, averaged over ten samples, the
uncorrected rule gives a KS distance of $0.0628$ against $0.0449$, and an integrated squared
error of $0.0334$ against $0.0154$.

\subsection{The window must shrink as the sample grows}

For the estimator to converge to the true density, the window must satisfy two classical
conditions: $h_n \to 0$, so that the smoothing eventually vanishes, and $n h_n \to \infty$,
so that the number of observations effectively contributing to each point still grows. The
first alone would leave a spike at every sample point; the second alone would leave a
permanently oversmoothed estimate.

The schedule used throughout this project,
\begin{equation}
h_n \;=\; \frac{h_1}{\sqrt{n}},
\label{eq:schedule}
\end{equation}
satisfies both: $h_n \to 0$ and $n h_n = h_1 \sqrt{n} \to \infty$. It reduces the choice of a
sequence of windows to the choice of a single number $h_1$, which is why it is convenient,
and Chapter~\ref{sec:window} is about how that number should be chosen. We keep this form
throughout, and Section~\ref{sec:schedule-cost} quantifies what it costs relative to the rate
that would be optimal.

\subsection{How an estimate is scored}
\label{sec:scoring}

Because the test distributions are known, every estimate can be compared against the truth.
We use two measures, and they answer different questions.

The \textbf{Kolmogorov--Smirnov distance} $\sup_x |\Fhat(x) - F(x)|$ scores the distribution
function. It has a natural reference point: the empirical distribution function, which uses
no smoothing at all, has expected KS distance $\sqrt{\pi/2}\,\ln 2 / \sqrt{n} \approx
0.8687/\sqrt{n}$. No estimator can reliably do much better than this, so it serves as a
statistical floor --- at $n = 500$ it is $0.0388$.

The \textbf{integrated squared error} $\int (\fhat - f)^2$ scores the density. This is the
measure that matters most here, since the density is what the assignment ultimately asks for,
and it is far less forgiving than the first. Integration is a smoothing operation, so errors
that are plainly visible in a density estimate can be almost invisible in its integral. We
will encounter a case where a badly chosen window costs a factor of two on the KS distance
and a factor of seven and a half on the integrated squared error; judging that window by the
first number alone would have declared it acceptable.

Two derived quantities are used when comparing selectors. The \textbf{oracle} is the window
that minimises the true error for the sample at hand. It is not a selector --- it uses the
answer --- and it is never part of any procedure we propose; it serves only as a
normaliser, since absolute errors are not comparable across distributions with different
shapes. The \textbf{relative efficiency} of a selector is its error divided by the oracle's,
a number at least $1$, where $1$ means it found the best window achievable with that
estimator on that sample. Reporting efficiencies rather than raw errors is what makes it
meaningful to average across a benchmark of very different densities.

\takeaway{The logistic kernel is chosen because its antiderivative is the sigmoid, which
makes the estimated distribution function exact, smooth and available in closed form, with
the density as its exact derivative. Classical bandwidth rules assume a unit-variance kernel
and must be rescaled by $\pi/\sqrt3$ before being used with it; omitting that correction
costs a factor of more than two on the density error. Density error is scored by integrated
squared error, which is the demanding measure, and selectors are compared by efficiency
relative to an oracle that is used only as a normaliser.}
