\begin{abstract}
We estimate the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of a known distribution from samples, using a
\textbf{Parzen Window} estimator, and we read the density off as the derivative. Working from a
\emph{known} distribution lets us score every estimate against the exact truth. Part~I builds the
estimator on the simplest case (a single Gaussian), one ingredient at a time: we choose the
\textbf{window size}, verify the fit, and study how the estimate behaves with the number of samples.
Part~II carries it to two Gaussian mixtures, tracking two sample budgets (a high-data setting and a
scarce-data one under 2000 samples).
\end{abstract}

\section{Setup and notation}

We draw samples $x_1,\dots,x_n$ from a known distribution and estimate its CDF $F(x)=\Pr[X\le x]$ and
its density $f=F'$. A \textbf{Parzen Window} estimator places one smooth window on each sample and
averages them. We use a logistic window, chosen so that the \emph{integral} of the window is the
logistic sigmoid $\sigma$; the estimated CDF is then the integral of the estimated density, available
in closed form:
\begin{equation}
  \fhat(x)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \frac1h\,\sigma'\!\Big(\frac{x-x_i}{h}\Big),
  \qquad
  \Fhat(x)=\int_{-\infty}^x \fhat
          =\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \sigma\!\Big(\frac{x-x_i}{h}\Big).
  \label{eq:parzen}
\end{equation}
The parameter $h>0$ is the \textbf{window size}: a small $h$ gives a sharp, noisy estimate, a large
$h$ a smooth, blurry one. We score an estimate by the \textbf{CDF gap}, the Kolmogorov--Smirnov
distance $\max_x|F(x)-\Fhat(x)|$ (the largest vertical gap between the estimated and true CDF, smaller
is better), and we also track the recovered density's peak height as a finer check.

\paragraph{Window-size selectors.} We compare several ways of choosing the window size; each uses only
the samples (never the truth), so each is deployable. \emph{Silverman's rule} (Silverman, 1986) is a
closed-form default, a spread estimate times $n^{-1/5}$, derived to be optimal for a Gaussian target
and so prone to over-smoothing multimodal ones. The \emph{variance-matched} window is a small
correction of our own: Silverman's constant assumes a unit-variance kernel, but the logistic window
has variance $\pi^2/3 \approx 3.29$ and so smooths about $1.8$ times more, so we rescale by
$\sqrt{3}/\pi \approx 0.55$ to match (a heuristic, not a named method). The \emph{adaptive} window
(Abramson, 1982) is per-point, narrower where the data is dense and wider in the tails, set as a pilot
window times $(\,\fhat_{\text{pilot}}(x_i)/g\,)^{-1/2}$ with $g$ the geometric mean of the pilot
density at the samples. \emph{Cross-validation} chooses the window that best predicts held-out
samples, in two variants: least-squares CV (Rudemo, 1982; Bowman, 1984), which minimizes an unbiased
estimate of the integrated squared error, and likelihood CV (Habbema et al., 1974), which maximizes
the leave-one-out log-likelihood. Both are accurate but cost $O(n^2 \times \text{candidates})$, which
is why we do not run them at the largest sample count. Full references are listed at the end.

\section{Part I: the single Gaussian \texorpdfstring{$N(0,1)$}{N(0,1)}}

\subsection{Choosing the window size}
\label{sec:gauss-window}

We start as simply as possible ($n=2000$) and add one ingredient at a time, each motivated by the
previous shortcoming (Table~\ref{tab:window}, Figure~\ref{fig:window}).

\begin{table}[H]
  \centering
  \caption{Window-size strategies on $N(0,1)$, $n=2000$.}
  \label{tab:window}
  \begin{tabular}{llc}
    \toprule
    Strategy & Window size & CDF gap \\
    \midrule
    fixed, trivial value      & $1.0$            & $0.157$ \\
    adaptive (per-point), pilot $=1.0$ & mean $1.0$ & $0.151$ \\
    \textbf{Silverman (data-driven)}   & $0.196$    & $\mathbf{0.020}$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A fixed window of size $1.0$ over-smooths.} The estimated density is far too wide and
  low (peak $0.21$ vs the true $0.40$): the logistic window of ``size one'' is itself broad. CDF gap
  $0.157$.
  \item \textbf{Making the window adaptive does not rescue a too-large pilot.} Per-point window sizes
  built from the fixed pilot barely shrink in the dense centre and only widen in the tails (they came
  out at mean $1.0$, spanning $0.93$ to $1.91$), so the over-smoothing remains. CDF gap $0.151$.
  \item \textbf{A data-driven global window size (Silverman) solves it.} Window size $0.196$ brings
  the gap to $0.020$; estimate and truth coincide. This is our estimator for the single Gaussian.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{How many samples do we need?}

We sweep the sample count for three window-size methods, the naive fixed window, Silverman, and
adaptive with a Silverman pilot, averaged over $5$ seeds (Table~\ref{tab:n},
Figures~\ref{fig:nsweep} and \ref{fig:nprog}). Three things stand out.

\begin{table}[H]
  \centering
  \caption{CDF gap on $N(0,1)$ for three window-size methods (mean over $5$ seeds).}
  \label{tab:n}
  \begin{tabular}{rccc}
    \toprule
    $n$ & fixed $1.0$ & Silverman & adaptive \\
    \midrule
    50     & 0.165 & 0.066 & 0.056 \\
    500    & 0.162 & 0.034 & 0.026 \\
    2000   & 0.158 & 0.019 & \textbf{0.012} \\
    20000  & 0.157 & 0.008 & \textbf{0.005} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A fixed window does not benefit from more samples.} The fixed window of size $1.0$
  never shrinks, so its CDF gap stays flat at about $0.16$ and its density peak is stuck near $0.21$
  no matter how much data we add. Only data-driven windows, which shrink as $n$ grows, improve.
  \item \textbf{The CDF is easy, even with very few samples.} For the data-driven windows the CDF gap
  is already small at $n=50$ ($\approx 0.06$). The reason is structural: the CDF is the \emph{integral}
  of the density (Eq.~\ref{eq:parzen}), and integration averages out the finite-sample noise, so the
  CDF estimate is far more forgiving than the density (whose peak fills in only with more data).
  \item \textbf{Adaptive is the best, now that it has a good pilot.} With a \emph{Silverman} pilot
  (rather than the too-large pilot of Section~\ref{sec:gauss-window}), the adaptive window beats
  Silverman at every sample count and reaches the true density peak the fastest. This is the estimator
  we carry to the mixtures.
\end{itemize}

\section{Part II: Gaussian mixtures}

We now move to two harder targets, a symmetric and an asymmetric two-mode mixture. From here we carry
\textbf{two operating points}: a high-data setting (\emph{best overall}, $n=20000$) and a scarce-data
budget (\emph{best under 2k}, $n=1000$, with $n=500$ also reported). Silverman tends to over-smooth the
valley between the modes, so we compare it against an \textbf{adaptive} (per-point) window size with a
Silverman pilot.

\section{Part III: a battery of random mixtures}

To check whether the bimodal conclusion generalises, we generate $10$ random Gaussian mixtures ($3$ to
$6$ modes, varied means and widths) and test every truth-free window-size selector on all of them, at
the two budgets. Cross-validation costs $O(n^2 \times \text{candidates})$, so it is run only at the
under-2k budget (it is impractical at $n=20000$); the others are cheap. Figure~\ref{fig:gallery} shows
the ten shapes.

\paragraph{The battery overturns the bimodal conclusion.} Across ten varied shapes the adaptive
window is no longer the winner: it is only middling (Table~\ref{tab:battery}), worse than
variance-matched at both budgets. Instead, \textbf{cross-validation} (least-squares or likelihood) is
the most accurate selector, about $0.028$ at the small budget, roughly $2.4$ times better than
Silverman, because it searches directly for the window that fits the data, which pays off on complex
multimodal shapes. Its cost makes it impractical at very large $n$, where the cheap
\textbf{variance-matched} window (a fixed $\times 0.55$ shrink of Silverman) is the best practical
choice ($0.015$). Silverman is consistently the weakest. The lesson is methodological as much as
numerical: a conclusion drawn from two distributions did not survive ten.

\section{Summary of the Parzen-estimation phase (Parts I to III)}

A Parzen Window estimates the CDF well across all the cases we tried, and the CDF is robust even at
tiny sample counts because it is an integral; the density and the multimodal structure are what demand
data. The right \emph{window size} is data-driven and depends on the budget: on the broad battery,
\textbf{cross-validation} is the most accurate where it is affordable (small samples), while the cheap
\textbf{variance-matched} window is the best practical choice at large samples. The adaptive window,
which looked best on two hand-picked bimodals, did not generalise. This consolidated estimator is what
the next phase uses: a network trained to learn the resulting CDF, with the density recovered as its
derivative.

\section{Part IV: learning the CDF with a network}

The Parzen estimate is already a usable CDF, so why fit a network to it? A network gives a compact,
differentiable model whose derivative is the density in closed form, and it is the object we will
later extend to higher dimensions. The network is trained \emph{only on the data points}: its inputs
are the $n$ samples $x_i$ and its labels are the Parzen CDF values $\Fhat(x_i)$ there, with no
collocation and no augmentation. We carry the two budgets directly, $n=1000$ and $n=20000$; on the
single Gaussian the target is the Silverman Parzen CDF.

\paragraph{How the estimates are read off, and the train/validation split.} The learned CDF is simply
the network evaluated on a dense \emph{equispaced} grid (used only for scoring and plotting, never for
training); the learned density is the network's derivative $\mathrm{d}F/\mathrm{d}x$, obtained by
automatic differentiation on that same grid. Training uses \emph{all} $n$ labelled data points (full
batch, a fixed number of epochs, with no validation split and no early stopping); we revisit a
validation split later, where it matters more (noisier targets).

\subsection{The simplest network}

We start as simply as possible: one hidden layer of width $16$, sigmoidal activations (so the output
has the smooth monotone S-shape of the logistic-window CDF, and its derivative is smooth), trained by
plain stochastic gradient descent at a \emph{fixed} learning rate, full batch. Adam and other
refinements are deferred to later steps. The density is the derivative of the trained network.

cannot match a target this sharp. Monotonicity is not an issue (no violations), as on the Parzen side.
This sets up the next step: replacing SGD with Adam, and then adding capacity and training, expecting
the network to close the gap to its target.

\section{Part V: mixtures, monotonicity, and the consolidated pipeline}

\subsection{The network on the mixtures}

Carrying the simplest-plus-Adam network to the two bimodals (target: the consolidated Parzen window
per budget) the network again reaches its Parzen target. Capacity matters only mildly: width 16
suffices at the small budget, more width helps at the large one, and too large a width can
under-train at a fixed budget. Two new effects appear: the density peak is harder to match than on
the Gaussian, and for the first time the unconstrained network produces a few percent of monotonicity
violations (symmetric mixture, large budget, width 16), which motivates enforcing the constraint.

\subsection{Enforcing monotonicity}

On that violating regime we compare the three routes (Table~\ref{tab:mono}). The soft penalty fails to
remove the violations and hurts the fit; the monotone-by-construction (Sill) network removes them but
costs accuracy; \textbf{downstream rectification} (take the trained CDF, apply a cumulative max,
rescale to $[0,1]$) removes them at negligible cost and, as a bonus, normalises the density to unit
mass. We adopt it.

\subsection{Checkpoint 1: the consolidated pipeline}

Putting it together (Figure~\ref{fig:ckpt}): samples $\to$ consolidated Parzen window $\to$ labels at
the data points $\to$ MLP (one hidden layer, sigmoid, Adam) $\to$ CDF and its derivative $\to$
rectification. On a ladder (single Gaussian, the two bimodals, the asymmetric trimodal) the pipeline
gives a monotone, unit-mass estimate that matches its Parzen target up to bimodal complexity. The one
failure is the asymmetric trimodal, whose sharp, well-separated, low-weight mode the width-32 network
blunts (CDF gap $0.088$ vs a target of $0.015$).

\subsection{What the univariate study establishes}

Across every case the network is a \emph{faithful learner} of its Parzen target, given an adequate
optimiser (Adam) and enough capacity and training for the target's sharpness. Monotonicity and unit
mass are guaranteed cheaply by downstream rectification. The accuracy ceiling is therefore the Parzen
target itself, set by the window size (Parts I to III), not the network. In one dimension this means
the network does not beat the kernel estimate; it faithfully reproduces it (Appendix~\ref{app:empirical}
even shows the bare empirical CDF can serve as the target). The network's real value is the
differentiable, compact, constraint-respecting functional form, which is what will carry to the
multivariate case.

\section{Learning the CDF directly from the empirical CDF}
\label{app:empirical}

This is a side experiment, outside the main pipeline (which trains the network on the Parzen CDF). We
asked whether the network even needs the Parzen step, by training the same network (one hidden layer,
width $16$, sigmoid, Adam) directly on the \emph{empirical CDF} of the samples,
$F_n(x_i) = (\mathrm{rank}-0.5)/n$, the rawest, parameter-free estimate of the true CDF from the data.
Single Gaussian, mean over five seeds.

\begin{table}[H]
  \centering
  \caption{CDF gap (KS) vs truth, mean over five seeds. The network trained on the empirical CDF is
  the most accurate, and beats even the raw empirical CDF it learns from.}
  \label{tab:empirical}
  \begin{tabular}{lcc}
    \toprule
    & $n=1000$ & $n=20000$ \\
    \midrule
    target: Parzen CDF (Silverman)     & 0.029 & 0.008 \\
    target: empirical CDF              & 0.029 & 0.006 \\
    net trained on Parzen              & 0.030 & 0.008 \\
    \textbf{net trained on empirical CDF} & \textbf{0.020} & \textbf{0.005} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{mlp_empirical_cdf.png}
  \caption{Network trained on the empirical CDF (green) vs on the Parzen target (blue), against the
  truth (black) and the raw empirical CDF (light gray), single Gaussian, seed $0$.}
  \label{fig:empirical}
\end{figure}

As targets the Parzen and empirical CDFs are about equally accurate. But the network trained on the
empirical CDF is the most accurate of all, about $30\%$ lower CDF gap than the network trained on the
Parzen target at both budgets, and it beats even the raw empirical CDF it learns from: its built-in
smoothness denoises the unbiased empirical target, whereas the network trained on the Parzen target
merely reproduces it and inherits Silverman's over-smoothing bias.

We do not adopt this as the pipeline. The test here is the single Gaussian only; on \emph{multimodal}
targets the empirical CDF carries no smoothness, so the network's recovered density (its derivative)
may turn wiggly, and there the Parzen pre-smoothing is expected to help. Confirming this on the
mixtures is left as future work.
